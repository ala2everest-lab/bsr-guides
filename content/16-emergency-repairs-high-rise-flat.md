Water coming through the ceiling, a boiler that dies in January, a cracked window twenty floors up: some problems cannot wait eight weeks for a decision. In a higher-risk building (HRB) in England, most building work needs approval from the Building Safety Regulator (BSR) before it starts, but the rules do allow genuinely urgent repairs to go ahead first. The catch is that the route is narrow, and it comes with paperwork that has to be done quickly. This guide explains what counts as an emergency repair, who must tell BSR and when, and what happens afterwards. It is general information, not legal advice or a building control decision.

## The short answer

- **You can start a genuine emergency repair without approval.** For an existing HRB, building work that consists only of emergency repairs can begin before BSR approval if it is not practicable to apply first ([regulation 10, SI 2023/909](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2023/909/regulation/10)).
- **You must tell BSR quickly.** GOV.UK says to notify BSR by the end of the first working day after the repair work starts ([GOV.UK](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/building-control-approval-for-higher-risk-buildings)).
- **You must regularise it afterwards.** Once the work is finished, you apply to BSR for a regularisation certificate.
- **It covers the urgent fix only.** Any wider work needed to deal with the problem goes into a normal building control approval application.

## First check: is it building work at all?

Before you think about the emergency route, check whether you need it. Plenty of urgent fixes are not notifiable building work, or are exempt, and can go ahead with no BSR involvement at all. GOV.UK says repair work that is not building work, or is exempt work, "can proceed without approval from BSR" ([GOV.UK](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/building-control-approval-for-higher-risk-buildings)).

Exempt work is listed in [Schedule 2 of the Higher-Risk Buildings Procedures Regulations](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2023/909/schedule/2). Examples relevant to a flat emergency include:

- replacing part of the existing cold water supply, such as a burst cold water pipe;
- replacing a bath, WC, basin, sink or shower, as long as the drainage and water work does not put anyone's health or safety at risk;
- most repairs and replacements of existing services.

The exemption has important gaps. GOV.UK excludes **heating systems, fuse boxes and glazing units** from the general repair and replacement exemption. Schedule 2 also excludes replacing a combustion appliance, so a replacement gas boiler is not exempt. Electrical work that adds a new circuit, replaces a consumer unit or alters circuits around a bath or shower is not exempt either.

For a fuller breakdown of what is and is not exempt in a flat, see Kitchen and bathroom refits in a high-rise flat.

## What counts as an emergency repair

The legal definition is short. Emergency repairs are "repairs to a building which are necessary to be carried out as a matter of urgency due to the risk to health, safety or welfare of persons in or about the building" ([regulation 10(2)](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2023/909/regulation/10)).

The [GOV.UK guidance](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/building-control-approval-for-higher-risk-buildings) adds examples of when an urgent risk may justify the route, such as where it:

- cannot be avoided or managed by putting temporary arrangements in place;
- occurs during planned building work and could not reasonably have been expected, detected or avoided;
- occurs because plant or other equipment in the building has broken down.

The guidance also sets limits:

- Emergency repairs "should not be carried out to fix problems that have occurred due to a lack of foresight or planning". A window that has been failing for three years is unlikely to qualify.
- Work needed to comply with a fire and rescue service enforcement notice is unlikely to count.
- Only the urgent repair is covered. "Any other building work needed to address the problem should be submitted in a building control approval application."

A practical test is to ask: what is the smallest piece of work that removes the immediate risk? That is the emergency repair. A full redesign, an upgrade or "while we're at it" extras are not.

## Repair or replacement?

The regulation says "repairs". No official source currently confirms whether replacing a whole item, such as a boiler, a window or a fire door, counts as a repair. Commentators have pointed out that the current route fits replacements poorly, and this was one of the reasons for the 2026 consultation (see below). If a replacement is unavoidable, record why a repair was not possible, and take advice before relying on the emergency route.

There is one other route worth checking for services work: competent person schemes. GOV.UK says you do not need building control approval for work carried out under a competent person scheme, but tells you to "ask the scheme provider if they cover building work on HRBs" ([GOV.UK](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/building-control-approval-for-higher-risk-buildings)). Get the answer in writing. For windows, the main glazing schemes have said they do not self-certify in buildings over 18 metres; see Replacing Windows in a High-Rise Flat.

## Telling BSR: what the notice must say

Under [regulation 10(1)](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2023/909/regulation/10), the client (the person the work is being done for) must give BSR a notice as soon as reasonably practicable after the work starts. GOV.UK sets a firmer target: by the end of the first working day after the repair work starts.

According to [GOV.UK](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/building-control-approval-for-higher-risk-buildings), the notice should:

1. describe the repair work;
2. explain the urgency and why it was not practical to apply for building control approval first;
3. describe any measures put in place to reduce the risk until the repair is finished.

You must also send a copy of the notice to the building's responsible person under the Regulatory Reform (Fire Safety) Order 2005. In most blocks that means the freeholder or managing agent, so tell them straight away; they will usually need to know about the leak or failure anyway.

Keep photos, the contractor's report and invoices from the start. You will need them for the next step.

## After the repair: regularisation

When the emergency work is complete, the client must apply to BSR for a regularisation certificate "as soon as possible" ([GOV.UK](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/building-control-approval-for-higher-risk-buildings)). Fees follow the BSR charging scheme.

BSR assesses the information provided. If the work does not meet the emergency repair criteria, GOV.UK says "the work is unauthorised and BSR will decide if they need to take any action". Starting non-emergency work without approval can be a criminal offence, which is why the record of why the work was urgent matters. See What happens if I start work without BSR approval? for the consequences.

In high-rise residential buildings, the building's principal accountable person also has to update the building safety case report to include the emergency repair. That is their duty, not yours, but it is another reason to keep them informed.

## Will the rules change?

Possibly. From 9 July to 3 September 2026 the government consulted on [widening "emergency repairs" to "emergency works", or creating a new route for "critical works"](https://www.gov.uk/government/consultations/improving-proportionality-and-building-safety-outcomes-in-building-control-emergency-repairs-under-the-higher-risk-building-regime). It also asked which emergency works could be self-certified under competent person schemes. As of 23 November 2026, the government has not published its response, and the current rules still apply.

## A quick checklist

1. **Make it safe first.** Isolate water, gas or power and use temporary measures where you can.
2. **Check whether the fix is building work at all,** or exempt. If it is not building work, or is exempt, no BSR notice is needed.
3. **If it is building work and cannot wait,** limit it to the urgent repair.
4. **Notify BSR** by the end of the first working day after work starts, and copy the responsible person.
5. **Keep evidence:** photos, reports and the reasons it could not wait.
6. **Apply for a regularisation certificate** once the repair is complete.
7. **Put any follow-on work** into a normal BSR application before it starts.

If you are planning the follow-on work and are not sure whether it needs BSR approval, the [BSR screener](https://bsrhrb.com) gives you a first view in a few minutes.
